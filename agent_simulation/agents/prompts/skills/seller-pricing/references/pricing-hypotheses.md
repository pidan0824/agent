Consider competing hypotheses, retaining unknown when evidence is sparse. These theories guide questions, not compulsory behavior or rational-price rules.

- Competition orientation: stable relative positioning or changes associated with peer quotes may motivate public comparisons; association does not prove the seller watched competitors.
- Demand orientation: own exposure and response history may be relevant, but conditional associations do not identify elasticity or establish a profit-maximizing response.
- Stable pricing: few observed changes may support continuing the quote with little search; this does not prove everyday-low-price positioning.
- Reference-price anchoring: own historical prices may be salient. Large historical variation may contradict a narrow reference-price hypothesis.
- Temporary promotion: repeated decreases and recoveries are possible evidence, but observation indices cannot verify calendar cycles or campaign intent.

Compare hypotheses against this seller's history rather than imposing a textbook strategy. Cost-plus pricing is not verifiable without costs. A concise final justification should identify the chosen tentative hypothesis, relevant observed facts and remaining uncertainty.
